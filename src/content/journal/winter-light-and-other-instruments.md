In winter, the light comes into my study at a low angle and stays for perhaps an hour. It lands on the edge of the desk, the spine of a book, the rim of a cup, and then it is gone. I have arranged my mornings around it more than I care to admit.

The Victorians had a word for this kind of light: *apricity*, the warmth of the sun in winter. It is a word that has fallen out of use, which seems appropriate. The thing it describes is not useful. It cannot be scheduled or stored. It arrives, briefly, and asks you to be present.

## The season as a lens

We tend to think of seasons as weather, and weather as an inconvenience. But a season is also an instrument. It changes the resolution at which the world is legible. Summer is broad and generous and a little exhausting. Autumn is elegiac, and knows it. Winter is narrow, precise, and honest. It strips the world down to its structure.

I have come to trust winter light more than summer light, for the same reason I trust pencil more than ink. It shows the grain. It does not flatter the surface. A room in winter is a room with its flaws visible, and therefore a room you can actually live in.

> The low sun makes an inventory of everything: the dust, the scratch, the place where the paint was touched up.

## What the body knows

There is a physiological story here — the shorter days, the change in melatonin, the ancient circuitry that tells us to slow down. But the physiological story is not the whole story. The body also knows something the calendar does not: that rest is not the absence of work. It is a different kind of work.

I used to fight the winter. I would keep summer hours in December, rising early into the dark, filling the day with lamps and screens, and then wonder why the writing felt thin. The writing felt thin because I was asking it to grow in the wrong season. Some thoughts need light. Others need the long, cold space in which to become themselves.

## A small inventory

This is not an argument for idleness, or for a romantic cult of the dark. It is an argument for allowing the season to set the terms now and then. In practical terms, it means small adjustments:

- Fewer plans, made later.
- More reading, less scrolling.
- One lamp instead of three.
- A walk at the hour when the light is best, even if it is short.
- A notebook close by, because the low light seems to loosen something.

None of this is a program. It is closer to a permission. The season has already made the argument. I am only agreeing with it.

## The instrument and the player

In the end, the season is not the composer. We still have to play. But an instrument that is played against its nature will always sound wrong, no matter how skilled the player. The task is not to master the winter. It is to learn what the winter is good at, and to bring that thing into the work.

The light leaves the edge of the desk. The cup is ordinary again. I turn on the lamp, which is not the same light, but is also not nothing. There is still an hour before dinner. There is still a sentence to be found. The instrument has been put down; the music is not over.
