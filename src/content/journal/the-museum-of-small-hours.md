There is a particular hour on a Sunday in late autumn when the light turns the colour of weak tea and the house seems to hold its breath. Nothing happens. The kettle cools. A fly negotiates the window. Somewhere a dog is barking at a neighbour it has known for years.

I have started to think of these hours as exhibits. Not because they are precious, but because they are endangered. We have built a world in which it is almost impossible to be unreachable for sixty consecutive minutes, and we have agreed, mostly without discussing it, that this is an improvement.

## The afternoon as a room

When I was a child, boredom was a physical condition. It had a texture — the nub of the carpet, the taste of a metal key, the sound of a clock that seemed to be deliberately slow. I remember lying on the floor of my grandmother's front room while the afternoon arranged and rearranged itself around me. Nothing was happening, and yet everything was.

I did not know then that I was learning something. I thought I was waiting for my life to start. In fact I was learning how to be a person in a room with no audience, no task, and no next thing.

We speak about attention as though it were a resource to be spent, like money or battery. But attention is closer to a place than a currency. It is somewhere you can live. The question is not how much you have, but whether you have furnished it, and whether you are willing to stay.

## Boredom as a door

The novelist and the child both know that boredom is not the absence of something. It is a threshold. Stand in it long enough and the mind begins to make its own weather: a half-remembered song, a plan you will never carry out, a sentence that arrives fully formed and then leaves before you can write it down.

> The capacity to endure a little emptiness is the beginning of every interior life.

We have made a virtue of filling the gap. The phone is a machine for abolishing the threshold. It offers, at every moment, a small, bright alternative to whatever is in front of us. And because it is always available, we stop noticing that we have chosen it. The gap closes so quietly that we forget there was ever a door.

I am not against the phone. I am against the assumption that every unclaimed minute is a failure of scheduling. Some minutes are supposed to be unclaimed. That is what makes them ours.

## A practice of noticing

Lately I have been keeping a small record of the unremarkable. Not a journal of events, but a list of impressions: the smell of cold stone, the sound of a bus braking in rain, the exact shade of a pear on the third day. It takes two minutes. It has changed the shape of my days more than any productivity system I have tried.

The practice is not about producing anything. It is about consenting to be where I am. To notice the pear is to admit that the afternoon is not merely a corridor to the evening. It is a room, and I am already in it.

There is a page in the notebook where I wrote, on a grey Tuesday in March:

- The radiator ticking like a slow insect.
- A woman on the phone saying *I know, I know, I know*.
- The particular yellow of the light before rain.

I cannot tell you what I did that day. I can tell you what it felt like to be alive in it. That seems like the better record.

## What the museum keeps

The small hours do not ask to be remembered. They accumulate anyway, in the body and in the ordinary language we use to describe ourselves. We are made of afternoons. Most of them will never appear in a photograph or a calendar. They are the sediment of a life, and they are enough.

So here is the proposal, offered without much hope of being adopted: leave one hour a week unaccounted for. Do not optimise it. Do not turn it into content. Sit in the weak tea light and let the fly negotiate the window. You may find that nothing happens. That is the exhibit. That is the whole museum.
