With the advancement of AI, hacking will increasingly shift away from the traditional emphasis on discovering software vulnerabilities and bugs towards exploiting the interactions between software systems, the sequences of operations they perform, and the assumptions built into their workflows. As applications become more sophisticated, interconnected, and increasingly dependent on APIs, third-party services, and autonomous agents, vulnerabilities will not necessarily exist within individual components but may emerge from how those components communicate and depend on one another. A system can have well-written code, strong encryption, and properly implemented authentication, yet still be vulnerable because of how legitimate operations are sequenced or how different components interpret and trust one another.

AI will accelerate this shift by lowering the barrier to understanding complex software ecosystems, automating reconnaissance, and identifying unusual relationships between systems, users, and permissions. Rather than relying exclusively on conventional exploits, attackers may increasingly look for ways to manipulate legitimate functionality, exploit misplaced trust between services, or take advantage of gaps between what a system is designed to do and what it can actually be made to do. The growing adoption of AI agents makes this particularly significant because these agents can interact with external tools, access resources, execute operations, and make decisions based on information they receive. This creates a new security dimension in which the integrity of instructions, permissions, context, and interactions becomes just as important as the integrity of the underlying software.

Perhaps the most significant shift, however, will be the growing importance of human behaviour and social engineering. As technical security improves, manipulating the people who operate, maintain, and interact with systems can become an attractive alternative to attacking the systems themselves. AI makes it easier to produce convincing impersonations, contextual deception, and highly personalised social engineering attempts at scale. Consequently, cybersecurity will increasingly require us to think beyond whether software can be compromised and consider whether the people, processes, relationships, and automated decisions surrounding that software can be manipulated. **The attack surface is no longer just the software; it is the entire ecosystem of interactions built around it.**
