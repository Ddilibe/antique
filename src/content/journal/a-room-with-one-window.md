The best room I ever wrote in had one window and nothing worth looking at. It was on the second floor of a borrowed house, at the back, facing a wall. The wall was brick, and beyond it, if you leaned, a strip of sky. There was a desk, a chair, a lamp, and a shelf with too few books on it.

I have since worked in rooms with better views. I have worked beside windows that opened onto gardens, rivers, and the slow theatre of a street. None of them produced as much. I have thought about why.

## Constraint as a door

A view is a kind of company. It offers itself to you continuously, and it is never quite boring enough to dismiss. The garden changes; the light on the water shifts; someone walks past. Each change is a small invitation to leave the sentence you are in.

A wall is different. A wall has nothing to offer. After the first week it becomes invisible, and then it becomes useful. The mind, finding no external entertainment, turns inward and begins to make its own. The wall is not an obstacle to the work. It is the condition of it.

> Every room is an argument about what deserves your attention. Choose the argument carefully.

## The myth of the ideal conditions

We like to imagine that the work is waiting for the right conditions: the right room, the right chair, the right amount of time, the right frame of mind. This is a comforting story because it means the failure to work is not our fault. It is the fault of the room.

But the people who make things rarely have ideal conditions. They have a constraint they have agreed to live with. A small desk. A noisy hour before the house wakes. A rule that they will write one page before checking the news. The constraint is not a compromise with the work. It is a technology for beginning.

## What one window teaches

A single window teaches proportion. You see the sky, but only a piece of it. You see the weather, but not the whole horizon. The small frame keeps the world at a manageable size. This turns out to be a useful way to think about everything: a life is not the whole sky. It is the piece you can actually see from where you are sitting.

I no longer believe in the ideal room. I believe in the room you can return to. The one with the wall, the lamp, the too-few books. The one that asks nothing of you except that you sit down.

## Leaving the room

Of course, a room with one window is not a life. Solitude has a limit, and the limit is a person. The work that comes out of the room has to go somewhere, into the hands of someone who is not you. Otherwise the constraint becomes a cage, and the wall becomes the only world.

So the room is a discipline, not a destination. You go in to concentrate, and you come out to be corrected. The window lets in just enough of the world to remind you that it is there. The wall keeps out enough to let you think. Between them, if you are lucky, something gets made.

The borrowed house is gone now. I still look for rooms at the back, facing walls. I have become suspicious of good views.
