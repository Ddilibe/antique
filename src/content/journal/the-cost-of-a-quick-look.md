I have a habit, when I open a book, of checking how many pages are left. I do it before I have read a single sentence. It is a small gesture, almost invisible, but it tells me something about the way I have learned to read: not as an immersion but as a distance to be closed.

We do this now with almost everything. The film is watched at 1.5 speed. The article is skimmed for the argument. The conversation is checked for its usefulness. The walk is tracked. There is nothing wrong with any of these in isolation. The trouble is that we have begun to treat the quick look as the only legitimate way to look.

## The glance and the gaze

There is an old distinction, from the psychology of perception, between a glance and a gaze. A glance is fast, targeted, and good at detecting change. A gaze is sustained, exploratory, and good at noticing relationship. Both are necessary. But they train different kinds of attention, and they produce different kinds of knowledge.

The glance tells you whether the shape in the grass is a threat. The gaze tells you what the field is made of. A culture that rewards glancing will still survive. It may even move faster. But it will slowly lose the vocabulary for everything that only the gaze can see: texture, contradiction, the slow emergence of meaning.

> We are not what we look at. We are what we look at long enough to be changed by.

## The economy of the quick look

The incentives are not mysterious. A quick look can be counted. A sustained look leaves no trace in a dashboard. When attention became a metric, it was inevitable that the short, measurable unit would win. The feed does not want your gaze. It wants your glance, again and again, because the glance can be interrupted, redirected, and sold.

This is not a conspiracy. It is an architecture. And like all architecture, it shapes the people who move through it. I do not think I am immune. I can feel the impatience in myself — a kind of low-grade itch when a paragraph takes longer than expected to arrive at its point. The itch is not a moral failing. It is a trained response.

## Relearning the long look

The remedy, if there is one, is not to renounce the quick look. It is to protect a few places where the gaze is still allowed. A book read without a target. A piece of music listened to without a second screen. A person listened to without preparing a reply.

I have found it useful to make the protection physical. The phone goes into another room. The book is paper, so that the page count cannot be checked by a swipe. The walk has no destination, or a destination so modest it does not require navigation. These are small, almost embarrassing measures. They work because they change the room, and the room changes the mind.

## What is lost in the skimming

I read an essay recently in the way I usually read now: quickly, for the argument. I could have summarised it in a sentence. I could have told you what it was against. But a week later I could not tell you what it was like to read. The sentences had left no impression, because I had not given them time to.

Then I read the same essay again, slowly, in the evening, with the lamp on. It was a different essay. Or rather, it was the same essay and a different reader. The argument was still there, but underneath it was something the quick look could never have found: a hesitation, a change of mind, a sentence that admitted more than it claimed.

That is the cost of the quick look. Not that we get the wrong answer, but that we stop asking the question that takes longer than an answer. And the question is usually the better part.
