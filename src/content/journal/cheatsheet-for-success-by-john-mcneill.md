I am in my twenties, and something that has been bothering me for quite a while is the idea of success. What does success actually look like? How do I see the bigger picture, and more importantly, how do I attain it? One thing that has contributed to this line of thinking is witnessing the costly mistakes people have made in their lives. I wouldn't want to make those same mistakes.

I mean, this kind of thinking can drive a person crazy. People often have a mental image of how they want their lives to turn out, but that image isn't always grounded in physical reality. They know what they want, but they don't necessarily have a realistic picture of how to get there. This can leave them in a state of zero movement but high momentum: constantly dreaming, thinking, and planning without taking meaningful action.

I can't lie; I sometimes fall into this category.

I have asked a few philosophers in my small circle to help me define success, but their answers have been utter rubbish. This has left me even more confused until I stumbled upon an interview between Jon McNeill, the former president of Tesla, and the host of *The Knowledge Project* podcast.

During the interview, McNeill indirectly introduced an interesting way of looking at success. Rather than becoming overly concerned with the bigger picture, he emphasized the importance of making smaller decisions and treating them as milestones along the journey to success.

It made me realize that success isn't necessarily one big destination waiting at the end of the tunnel. It is also the accumulation of smaller decisions made along the way. The important thing is understanding what those small decisions should look like and how they contribute to the eventual outcome.

McNeill calls his approach the **cheat sheet for decision-making**.

### Jon McNeill's Cheat Sheet for Decision-Making

1. Begin with the end.
2. Identify the criteria required for success.
3. Create the environment necessary for those criteria.
4. Consider the impact of the decision.

These four principles provide a framework for making decisions that lead to successful and desirable outcomes.

The first step, *begin with the end*, involves having a clear idea of what you want to achieve. What should the outcome of the decision look like? What are you ultimately trying to accomplish?

Once you have established that, the second step is to identify the criteria required to achieve that outcome. In other words, what conditions, constraints, or constants must be in place for success to become possible?

The third step involves creating an environment where those criteria can thrive. It is not enough to know what needs to happen; you must also establish the conditions that make it possible.

Finally, the fourth step is to visualize the impact of the decision. What happens when you make this decision? How does it affect the desired outcome, and what are its possible consequences?

### My Takeaway

The biggest takeaway for me is that I might have been looking at success from the wrong perspective. Perhaps I have been too focused on the destination without paying enough attention to the decisions that will get me there.

Instead of constantly asking myself what success looks like in the grand scheme of things, I should start by asking:

**What does success look like in this particular scenario?**

Then, I can work through McNeill's four steps to make better decisions.

Perhaps the bigger picture becomes clearer when I learn to get the smaller decisions right.
